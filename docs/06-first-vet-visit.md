# 06 · The First Vet Visit

Most rescues ask you to see a vet within the first week — some contracts
require it within a few days. This chapter gets you ready: what to bring,
what to ask, and what a first visit usually involves.

*Reminder: this guide is education only and is never veterinary advice. Your
vet makes the medical calls.*

## Booking the visit

- Ask the rescue for **their vet** or a partner clinic. They usually know
  who's friendly to new rescues.
- Say when booking: *"Newly adopted rescue, first-week exam. I have records
  from the shelter to forward."*
- Book a morning slot if you can — the clinic is calmer and you have the
  whole day afterward if something needs follow-up.
- For cats: bring the carrier, covered with a towel. For dogs: leash and a
  well-fitted collar/harness.

## What to bring (the paperwork kit)

1. **Adoption paperwork** — contract, adoption date, fee receipt
2. **Medical records from the rescue** — vaccines, deworming, treatments,
   FeLiV/FIV results (cats), heartworm status (dogs)
3. **Microchip number** and registration company — ask the vet to confirm it
   scans and reads correctly
4. **Your first-week log sheet** — filled in. Food intake, water, potty, sleep,
   mood, plus any accidents. Vets can tell you things from one filled week
   that they can't from "he seems fine, I think?"
5. **The current food** — a photo of the bag's front label is enough
6. **A list of what worries you** — genuinely, write it down. The anxiety
   vanishes in the exam room, and you forget whether the sneezing started
   Tuesday or Saturday.
7. A **towel or mat** for the exam table (nonslip comfort) and treats — many
   clinics say yes to a scatter of treats; ask, don't just do.

## The question list

Nobody remembers everything mid-appointment. Bring this page and pick your
questions:

**Status and next steps**

- [ ] Based on the records, what's done versus still needed? (vaccines,
      boosters, deworming, heartworm/flea prevention timeline)
- [ ] What's the schedule from here — and when is the next visit?
- [ ] Does the pet need to be quarantined from my other animals, and for how
      long?

**Food and body**

- [ ] Given what the rescue fed, how much should this animal weigh, and are
      we close?
- [ ] How much should I feed, and of what? (carry the bag label photo)
- [ ] Any dental care advice appropriate at this age?

**Behavior and training**

- [ ] Is this behavior pattern consistent with normal rescue adjustment?
      (describe hiding/eating patterns in words — the log sheet helps)
- [ ] Do you have a recommended trainer or behaviorist for anything like
      guarding or fear? (Ask *now*; get it before you need it.)

**Practical housekeeping**

- [ ] Confirm microchip is registered and to whom.
- [ ] What are your emergency/after-hours instructions — where do I go, who
      do I call at 2 a.m.?
- [ ] What's your estimate for routine next-year care, roughly? (Good for
      budgeting; ask about **pet insurance** pros/cons if you're considering
      it — a vet's opinion beats an insurance ad.)

**If anything's been odd this week**

- [ ] Describe the specific symptom and ask directly: *"Is this a
      wait-and-see, or does it jump the queue?"*

## What to expect at the exam

A first rescue exam is typically bigger than a routine checkup: temperature,
weight, ears, eyes, teeth and gums, heart and lungs, abdomen, skin and coat,
lymph nodes; sometimes a fecal test even with no symptoms (parasites are
common in rescues, being screened beats guessing); possibly baseline bloodwork
depending on history and age. Cats may get re-checked for FeLiV/FIV if the
shelter's results are missing.

The visit often ends with a written plan (or an emailed summary). If it
doesn't, ask: *"Can you email me the checklist of what we decided?"*

## After the visit

- File the visit summary with your adoption papers — a photo is fine.
- Update your log sheet's "vet" column: what was done, when, what's next.
- Follow the plan; note side effects of anything new (some medications upset
  stomachs) — the log sheet is again your best evidence.
