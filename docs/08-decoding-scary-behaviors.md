# 08 · Decoding Scary Behaviors

Your new pet will do things in week one that look alarming. Most of them are
normal-fearful, not dangerous, and almost all of them mean the same sentence:

> **"I am new here and I don't know what's safe yet."**

This chapter translates the scary stuff. And it repeats the standing rule:
this guide is education only — when in doubt, your vet or a behavior
professional makes the call.

## The fear ladder: freeze, flee, fight

Scared animals pick one of four responses, roughly in order of escalating
distress:

1. **Hide** — withdraw, go small, go silent
2. **Freeze** — stop moving, stare, "statue mode"
3. **Flee** — bolt, scramble, even toward danger in panic
4. **Fight** — hiss, growl, snap (a *last* resort, usually after 1–3 fail)

Reading which rung your animal is on is more useful than memorizing specific
behaviors — a locked, silent animal is often more distressed than a
vocalizing one.

## The dictionary

### Hissing, growling, or snapping

These are **distance-increasing signals** — "please back off." They are
actually *good* news compared to silence: the animal is communicating, not
matching an invisible threshold. The response is never punishing; it's
**giving space**. Step back, lower your body, be quiet, and offer a treat
tossed from a distance if they'll take it.

- **Cats hiss and growl readily in weeks one and two** — very common, almost
  always the fear response, not aggression to fix.
- **A dog that growls** is being honest; punishing growl taught many experts
  that removing the warning (and snapping next time, unannounced) was the
  safer strategy to them. Never punish the growl; change the situation and
  get professional help if it escalates.

A **snap or bite that breaks skin** is a same-day medical and behavioral
priority — for the human first (wash the wound, you know the drill) and then
behavior planning; you'll want your vet's perspective.

### Freezing / statue mode

"Stop the world, I want to get off." Common on leash for new dogs (harness +
leash + street = a lot). Response: stop moving, wait, crouch, coax with a
soft voice, and reward any un-freezing. Never drag them forward. The walk
ends when they walk; that's fine.

### The flattened crouch / whale eye / tucked tail (dogs)

- Flattened crouch, tucked tail, "half-moon eye" (whites showing): classic
  fear. Give space; check what in the room just happened.
- "Whale eye" while holding a bone means *do not reach* — see guarding below.

### Rolling over, belly-up

Especially cats: often **not an invitation** to touch the belly. It can be
defensive (weapons-loaded). For dogs, some love belly rubs; watch for stiff
vs. loose bodies. If you're new to leaning over a new pet, wait a week and
let *them* decide contact.

### Moved litter box / stopped eating when you moved bowls

Cats in particular re-audit their world daily. Bowl moves, box moves,
furniture rearrangement — these reset map-learning. Don't do it all in one
week. If something must move, do one thing per few days and keep food and
potty locations consistent.

### Not eating when guests visit

Guests are loud strangers to a week-one animal. Feed on schedule, keep calm,
no guests this week anyway ([chapter 7](07-introducing-family-and-pets.md)).

### Sudden "regression"

A pet that was calm day 3 and hides again on day 5 — startling noises,
visitors, a new smell, a moved bowl. Not lost progress; progress isn't a
staircase, it's more of a zigzag uphill. Return to basics:
quiet, routine, food, patience.

### Zoomies, midnight, destructive sniffing, the 5th bathroom break

Nervous energy has to go somewhere. Play more deliberately in the daytime
(structured play burns it clean); expect accidents before meals settle.

## Resource guarding (the pattern to watch calmly)

Growling, stiffening, or reaching-a-paw-over-food when someone approaches
food bowls, treats, beds, or *you*.

In week one: mostly just **note it**. Don't test it, don't take things away
to "prove dominance," and don't hand-feed with fingers close if there's
guarding. Feed in a quiet spot, walk away, back off when the signal comes,
and log it. If it's frequent or escalates, put a certified trainer or
behaviorist on the calendar — guarding responds well to early work and badly
to improvised punishment.

## When scary is *medical* scary

Some frightening behavior is pain or illness showing through. Red flags
worth a [vet-call](09-call-the-vet-or-wait.md):

- Behavior change **plus** physical signs: not eating, lethargy, hiding in
  unusual places (not their chosen den — e.g., cat hiding in the cold
  bathtub), yelping when moved or touched in a specific spot
- A pet who was fine and suddenly is a different animal
- Limping; dragging a leg; crying out at night with no visible cause
- Disorientation, bumping into things, circling one direction, head tilt

You know your week-one baseline. Anything that breaks it *with* those physical
signs is professional-triage territory, not guide-reading territory.

## The general calibration

| You see | Most likely meaning | Your move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ear-pinning, hissing | Fear, communicating | Space + time; no punishment |
| Crouching, freezing | Overwhelmed | Slow everything; be boring |
| Growl over food/bones | Guarding (usually fear under it) | Back off; log; pro plan later |
| Won't eat for a couple of days then eats | Classic week one | Watch the pattern; vet if it extends |
| Sudden extreme change + physical signs | Possible illness | Vet. Same day. |
