# 02 · The Quiet Safe Room

The single most useful thing you can do for a new rescue pet is give them one
small, boring, quiet room that belongs to them. Not the whole house. One room.

## Why a safe room works

To a newly rescued animal, your home is enormous, smelly, and full of unknown
sounds. A small room lets them scan less, hide less far, and start to think
*this corner is mine and nothing bad happened here yet*.

- **For cats:** the safe room is basically mandatory for the first days or
  weeks. Bathrooms and spare bedrooms work well.
- **For dogs:** a "safe room" may be a quiet corner or gated-off room with
  their crate set up inside; the principle is the same.

## Setting it up (checklist style)

Full printable version: [safe-room checklist](checklists/safe-room-checklist.md).

1. **Pick a low-traffic room.** Not the hallway bathroom everyone uses, not
   the kitchen. A spare bedroom, office, or guest bath is ideal.
2. **Remove or secure hazards.**
   - Indoor plants that are toxic (common ones: lilies — *deadly to cats*,
     pothos, philodendron, sago palm)
   - Dangling cords and blind strings
   - Open trash cans
   - Anything breakable at tail height, basically
   - For dogs: shoes, remotes, medication, and gum (xylitol is extremely
     dangerous to dogs)
3. **Set up stations, spread apart.**
   - Food, water, litter (cats), and bed in *different* spots — they don't like
     dining next to the toilet
   - Water away from heat/AC vents
4. **Add at least two hiding spots.** A cardboard box on its side, a covered
   cat bed, a blanket draped over a chair. Cats want to see without being seen;
   dogs want their back to a wall.
5. **Make it comfortable.** Warm in winter, cool in summer, no drafts.
6. **Lower the lights.** Soft light helps a shut-down animal feel unseen.
7. **Close the windows** and keep the door shut. New escape-proofing, not
   fresh air, is the priority right now.

## Door discipline (the rule that saves week one)

The safe room only works if the door stays shut. Tell everyone in the house:

> "The door stays closed. We go in and visit; the pet comes out when *they*
> decide, weeks from now."

The #1 new-pet escape story is: someone opened the door, the cat bolted, and
the family spent three days coaxing it out from under a different bed. The
second #1 story is the cat that is never saw again. Closed door. Every time.

## It's okay to sit in there and do nothing

A good week-one activity: go into the safe room, sit on the floor, scroll your
phone or read a book, and ignore them completely. Your calm presence on the
floor at their level does more teaching than any attempt to touch them. When
they approach you — they will, eventually — let them; don't reach.

## Progressing out of the safe room

Move at *their* speed:

1. They hide less and eat normally →
2. They explore when you're in the room →
3. **They want the door open** (pacing, staring, pawing at it) and come back
   to the room *voluntarily* → then start letting them explore one more room
   at a time, with the safe room still available forever.

The safe room never gets taken away. It becomes "their room" — the one place
in the house they can always retreat to.
