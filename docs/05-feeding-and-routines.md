# 05 · Feeding and Routines

Food is the fastest route to trust — and the most misunderstood. Rescue week
is not the week to experiment.

## Rule zero: keep the food they're on (for now)

Their stomach is already stressed. A sudden food switch on top of everything
else can mean days of loose stools. So:

1. **Feed exactly what the rescue fed** for at least the first week.
2. If you plan to switch brands, do it *later*, slowly, over 7–10 days:
   roughly 75/25 old/new, then 50/50, then 25/75, then full new. Watch the
   poop; it votes.
3. Ask the rescue the exact brand *and* formula. "Purina" is half an answer.

If the rescue fed something you can't find or won't feed, ask them what's
closest; switch slowly anyway. When in doubt about diet fundamentals, ask
your vet — food conversations belong in [chapter 6](06-first-vet-visit.md)'s
question list.

## How much and when

- Follow the bag's weight chart *as a starting point*, not gospel. Ask the
  rescue what they fed and how much; ask your vet at the first visit.
- **Cats:** 2 meals a day is fine for adults; kittens need several small
  meals. Free-feeding (dry food down all day) is common and okay for many
  cats, but measured meals help you notice appetite changes — which in week
  one is exactly what you're monitoring.
- **Dogs:** 2 meals a day for adults is standard; puppies need 3 or more.
  Twenty minutes down, then pick up; free-feeding makes house-training harder
  and appetite changes invisible.

## Water

- Fresh, always, in the room and wherever they're allowed.
- Some new dogs drink enormously after car-ride stress; it's usually fine.
  A sudden *extreme* increase lasting days is a vet-call topic.

## Treats in week one

Treats are training currency — used in pea-sized doses for rewarded calm,
carrier acclimation, and coming out of hiding. They should be, at most, about
10% of daily calories. If a pet isn't eating meals, don't sugar-coat the
fasting with a buffet of treats; the fasting itself is the signal.

## Routines do the heavy lifting

Rescue animals relax under predictability, not freedom. A day that repeats:

| Time | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Morning | Same place, same bowl, same phrase: "breakfast" |
| Water check | Quick top-up, minute-long |
| Potty loop (dog) | Same door, same leash, same route |
| Afternoon | Quiet hours; humans doing boring human stuff |
| Evening | Dinner, short play for dogs / wand toy for cats |
| Night | Same bed, same lights-out phrase |

Pick your anchors (feed times, walk times, lights-out) and defend them for a
full week. That's it. The routine itself is the security blanket.

## Sleep

- Expect weird sleep for a few nights. It normalizes as routine lands.
- Cats sleep 12–16 hours a day as adults regardless of mood; a sleeping cat
  is not a problem unless it's *also* not eating or using the box.
- Dogs: keep the crate/bed in "their" spot from day one; moving beds around
  resets the learning.

## What to log

Use the [first-week log sheet](checklists/first-week-log-sheet.md):

- Did they eat? How much of the portion?
- Water — normal drinking, barely, or lots?
- Poop and pee — frequency, texture (it's weird, we know; it's clinical gold)
- Sleep — where, roughly how long
- Energy/mood — one line per day: "hiding but came out for dinner"

Week one ends with a page that tells you — and better still, your vet — the
shape of your pet's new normal.
